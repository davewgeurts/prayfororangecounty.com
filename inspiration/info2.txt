3. What the lore actually says
The most consistently documented rule is simple: stop talking and stop the music while passing the spot. The 2006 account describes both requirements; the 2014 account connects breaking the rule with injury. 

Individual versions differ. In the 2022 discussion, some participants describe rolling down the windows or observing the silence outside ski season. Other local participants say they had never heard of the custom. That supports describing it as a tradition among some canyon users, rather than something universally practiced by Utah skiers. 

The proposed origin stories fall into several categories:

Explanation in circulation    What the evidence supports
Respect for the mountain    The Wasatch article records an explanation that silence acknowledges the mountain where the road cuts through it. This is a reported interpretation, with no established founding event.
A memorial to people who died    The same article recounts versions involving fatal crashes or road-construction deaths. It does not substantiate a particular incident as the origin.
Keeping the driver focused    Quiet at a difficult corner is offered as a practical explanation. It remains a hypothesis.
A custom brought from Mount Hood    Borrowing from Oregon is suggested, but no documented person, date, or chain of transmission is provided.
Those are competing explanations recorded in the local article, rather than verified historical conclusions. 

The 2022 discussion also includes a more specific story about two workers killed while using dynamite. I found no corroborating names, dates, or accident report connecting that account to the ritual. It should remain an unverified story told about the place.

Oregon has a separate, well-documented Silent Rock on Highway 26 near Rhododendron, with its own silence tradition and competing origin legends. The Atlas Obscura entry is explicitly about that location. Its stories cannot establish what happened in Utah. 

There is even a concrete example of the locations getting mixed up: a 2024 Oregon Reddit thread about Mount Hood used the Utah photograph from the Wasatch article. A commenter identified the mismatch, and the original poster acknowledged grabbing the wrong image from a search. Photo provenance will matter if you use web references for the experience. 

Prayer Rock in Farmington
There is also a climbing area actually listed as Prayer Rock on Mountain Project, in Farmington/Davis County. That is a geographically different site from the Big Cottonwood Canyon location discussed here. 

7. Where a deeper historical answer might still be hiding
The searchable public record leaves several questions open: the original name, who introduced the rule, its starting date, and whether a snow-prayer version circulated in a particular group.

Two archival routes look useful.

Big Cottonwood Canyon Historical Society / Brighton Institute
The Brighton Institute’s archive page lists local collections, including the BCCA and Nickerson collections. It explains that the underlying materials are physical records kept at the Brighton Fire Station, with access coordinated through Kara John, whose published research contact is karajohn@brighton.utah.gov. 

A useful inquiry would include all the recorded names—Silent Rock, Silent Corner, Silent Curve, Silent Turn, Prayer Rock, and the single “Gates of Ullr” recollection—and ask for dated mentions, recollections of who taught the custom, and any specific evidence behind the road-worker story.

University of Utah’s Ski and Snow Sports Archives
The Brighton Ski Resort records, collection ACCN 1838, contain historical accounts, resort material, and newspaper clippings. Particularly relevant catalog entries are the histories in Box 1, folders 12 and 14–15, the news clippings in folder 18, and the 1999–2000 ski-school yearbook in folder 5. These are promising places to check for older language or recollections; I have not established that they mention the rock.
